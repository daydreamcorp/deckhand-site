Twelve testers. Fourteen days. Then Deckhand can launch.

Google Play doesn't let a new developer publish anything until at least twelve people have run the app in a closed test for fourteen days in a row. That test is the only thing left between Deckhand and its public release — so this is the honest ask, with the deal written out in full.

What you'd do

  1. Open the app once a day, for fourteen days. Five minutes is plenty. The days have to be consecutive — that's Google's rule, not ours, and a gap restarts the clock for everybody.
  2. Work through the day's quests. Five arrive each morning, worth 100 points together. They're what tells you what to test, so you never have to open the app and wonder what to do with it.
  3. Say what broke. One line by email is fine. Finding the rough edges is the whole reason the test exists.

What you get

  • Three months of Elite, free, when Deckhand launches. The top tier — unlimited tiles, pages and paired computers, and every action type there is: shell commands, OBS scenes, webhooks, the macro recorder, dials and faders.
  • Every paid feature unlocked from day one. Test builds run at Elite already, so nothing is behind a paywall while you're testing. You're not evaluating a crippled free tier.
  • No ads. None. The ad surface only exists on the free tier, and you're not on it.
  • A say in what ships. Twelve people is a small enough room that what you report actually changes the product before anyone else sees it.

You get quests, not homework.

Five quests land each morning, drawn from a catalogue of forty daily objectives and thirty-two badges spanning eleven areas of the app — pairing, deck editing, macros, geofence triggers, skins, the desktop side, latency. What you need

  • An Android phone or tablet. The closed test runs on Google Play, so Android only for now — the iOS build isn't in testing yet.
  • A Windows, macOS or Linux computer on the same network. Deckhand controls that computer; without one there's nothing to press.
  • A Google account email. Play invites are tied to the exact account you'll download with — a different address from the one on the phone is the single most common way enrolment fails.
  • Play auto-update left on. Test builds expire about a week after they're compiled and are replaced mid-window. If yours goes stale you're locked out and the cohort's clock resets.
  • Roughly five minutes a day, fourteen days running.

Straight answers before you commit

A test build behaves differently from the app that eventually ships. Here's every way that's true, up front.

Is my usage tracked during the test?

Yes, and you should know exactly how before you sign up. Test builds collect anonymous usage analytics — which screens you open, which controls you use, connection quality, quest progress — and unlike the public release, the off-switch in Settings is hidden for testers outside the EEA and UK. A twelve-person funnel stops being readable if a few people quietly opt out, so for the closed test it's on.

What's never collected: the contents of your deck, your tile labels, what your buttons actually do, or anything you type into the app. Layout tracking is off and the privacy policy commits to that in writing. When Deckhand reaches production the toggle comes back for everyone.

Why can't I take a screenshot?

Test builds block screenshots, screen recording and casting, and show a blank thumbnail in the app switcher. The deck editor, the skins and the pricing surface aren't announced yet, and the closed test hands a complete build to twelve people outside the company.

It's a testing-phase measure and nothing more — it comes off before the public release. If you need to show us something, describe it or point a second camera at the screen; that's genuinely fine.

What happens if I miss a day?

Your streak resets and the gold badge is out of reach, but you're not thrown out — keep going and the fourteen days still count toward the reward. Tell us if life gets in the way rather than going quiet: what actually costs us is a tester who disappears without saying so, because Google measures continuous opted-in days across the whole group.

When exactly do I get the three months of Elite?

On release. Elite can't be granted against a store listing that doesn't exist publicly yet, so the free three months start when Deckhand reaches production and are applied to the same Google account you tested with. In the meantime the test build already runs at Elite, so you lose nothing by waiting.

How does the extra month for referring work?

One extra month of Elite for every tester you bring who is approved and actually starts testing, on top of the three months the test itself earns you. There's no cap and no code to enter — email us their name and Google-account email, or have them say who sent them when they write in.

Two things worth knowing before you start asking around. Every referral is read and approved by hand before an invite goes out, so a name is a nomination rather than a guarantee. And the month is credited when they enrol and open the app for their first day, not when their email arrives — invites are tied to a specific Google account, so referring yourself from a second address doesn't get past us.

Can I quit partway through?

Yes, at any point, by leaving the test from the Play listing or by telling us. No obligation and no hard feelings — an email saying you're out is worth a lot, because it tells us to recruit a replacement instead of waiting on someone who isn't coming back.

Does it cost anything?

No. The phone app and the desktop app are both free to install, there's nothing to pay during the test, and no card is collected at any point. The desktop installers aren't code-signed yet, so Windows and macOS will warn you on first run — the install steps further down cover exactly what to click.

After the download — one step per platform

WindowsYour system
  1. Open the downloaded Deckhand-Installer-windows-x86_64.exe.
  2. Windows will show “Windows protected your PC”. Click More info, then Run anyway.
  3. The installer runs without admin rights and puts Deckhand under your own user account.

That warning appears because the build isn’t code-signed yet, not because anything is wrong with it. A signing certificate is on the list.

macOS — Apple SiliconYour system
  1. The download is a plain binary with no file extension, so double-clicking it won’t work. Open Terminal.
  2. Make it executable and clear the download quarantine: cd ~/Downloads chmod +x Deckhand-Installer-macos-arm64 xattr -d com.apple.quarantine Deckhand-Installer-macos-arm64
  3. Run it: ./Deckhand-Installer-macos-arm64

Both commands are needed because the build isn’t signed or notarised yet — without them macOS reports the file as damaged, which it isn’t. Apple Silicon only; there’s no Intel build.

Linux — AppImageYour system
  1. Mark it executable — a freshly downloaded AppImage does nothing at all until you do: cd ~/Downloads chmod +x Deckhand-Installer-linux-x86_64.AppImage
  2. Run it: ./Deckhand-Installer-linux-x86_64.AppImage
  3. If it complains about FUSE, either install libfuse2 or run it without FUSE: ./Deckhand-Installer-linux-x86_64.AppImage --appimage-extract-and-run

Your file manager can set the executable bit too — Properties → Permissions → “Allow executing file as program”.

One installer, no admin/root needed — it places Deckhand under your user account, adds a Start Menu / Applications launcher, and registers it to start with your session. The Windows and Linux builds were rebuilt most recently; if something looks off, that's useful to hear about.
